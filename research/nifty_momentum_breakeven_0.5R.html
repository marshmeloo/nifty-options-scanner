NIFTY Momentum: The Breakeven@0.5R Exit Rule

The exit logic running live on the scanner today — arm a breakeven stop once a trade reaches 0.5R, and keep watching every armed contract to its original 2R target even after it closes. Six years of real market data, real transaction costs, real measured bid-ask spread.

Prepared 2026-08-14 Universe NIFTY 50 index options Window 2020-08-03 → 2026-08-04 (1,491 trading days, 10,660 trades) Live in production
Executive Summary

The NIFTY momentum scanner buys calls or puts on an OI/IV/PCR/VWAP signal and exits one of three ways: hits a fixed 2R target, gets stopped out, or — as of today, 2026-08-14 — arms a breakeven stop the instant a trade reaches +0.5R and closes near entry if price falls back instead of letting a trade that was once winning round-trip into a real loss. Replayed against six years of reconstructed real market data (10,660 trades) with real statutory costs and real measured bid-ask spread on every leg, this exit logic returns +₹36,79,201 net — a +736% cumulative return on a flat ₹5,00,000 base — through a 16.75% drawdown (₹83,764) that recovered in roughly three months.

21.6% of all trades (2,299 of 10,660) were closed by this rule rather than their original target or stop. Across the full six years, it never once cut off a trade that would have gone on to hit target — every one of the 2,505 real winners stayed a winner. That is a property of this specific dataset, not a guarantee, which is why the live system now keeps a separate, non-P&L "shadow" record on every breakeven-closed contract, watching it forward to the original 2R target so the question keeps getting a real answer instead of being trusted forever on a backtest. See §5 for how that works.

01 — The StrategyHow the scanner decides what to buy, and when it gets out

The live process (main_live.py) polls the NIFTY option chain every 30 seconds during market hours and runs every strike through the same five-stage pipeline: scan → signal → plan → risk → decision. Nothing in the pipeline ever places a broker order — it is a decision-support system the operator acts on manually.

Entry signal

A strike is flagged when its own option chain shows a coherent read: OI buildup classification (long buildup / short covering / short buildup / long unwinding, from OI and premium change over a rolling window), IV rank relative to the chain's own cross-sectional distribution, chain-wide put-call ratio, and VWAP deviation. Each contributing signal adds to a composite score; a candidate needs a raw score that survives a recent win-rate adjustment and clears MIN_CONVICTION_SCORE_TO_TRACK = 5.0 before it becomes a tracked trade — the large majority of scanned strikes never reach this bar.

Stop, target, and the breakeven arm

The stop is not a flat percentage. It is 1.5 × ATR(14) of the underlying, converted to premium points via the contract's own delta, then clamped to a 15–40% band of entry premium. The target is a fixed 2R — twice the stop distance. A trade closes the first of four ways to trigger, checked in this order every cycle:

  1. Target hit — outright win at 2R.
  2. Breakeven-armed stop — the trade has at some point reached +0.5R favorable excursion and price has now fallen back to entry.
  3. Original stop hit — the fallback for trades that never got going, since an armed trade's price must cross back through entry before it could even approach the original stop.
  4. End of day — forced close, no overnight hold.

Position size is min(⌊(TOTAL_CAPITAL × 1%) ÷ (risk-per-lot)⌋, MAX_LOTS_PER_TRADE) — capital deployed per trade is capped at roughly 1% of the ₹5,00,000 base, and total concurrent exposure across all open positions is capped at 20%.

02 — Data & MethodWhere these numbers come from

The full option chain cannot be recorded further back than the live system has existed. To get six years of history instead of a few weeks, every cycle in this study is reconstructed from Dhan's Expired Options Data endpoint — real recorded OHLC, OI, and IV at 5-minute granularity for every strike within ±10 offsets of ATM, replayed through the exact same scanner, plan generator, and risk checker the live process runs. This is not a simulation of a different strategy; it is the strategy's own code — including the breakeven-arm exit now live in production — fed real historical market data it never got to see live.

Every trade in this note is walked forward tick-by-tick through the recorded chain to its actual outcome — target hit, breakeven stop, original stop, or forced end-of-day close — never assumed. This exact CSV was regenerated from a completely fresh scanner run on 2026-08-14, not reloaded from any earlier cached file, specifically so every figure below can be checked against a single, current, from-scratch source rather than numbers assembled across several runs on different days.

What is charged

  • Statutory costs — STT (sell side, on premium), exchange transaction charges, SEBI turnover fees, stamp duty, brokerage, and GST on all of the above — a real Indian discount-broker cost stack, not a round number.
  • Bid-ask spread — measured, not assumed, from five days of real recorded order books, banded by premium level because a ₹15 far-OTM contract's spread is nothing like a ₹150 near-ATM one's (0.82% median under ₹20 premium vs. 0.23–0.30% from ₹50 up, worsening again past ₹200). Charged on both legs of every trade — entry and exit — at each leg's own premium band, using the median scenario throughout this note (the p75/p90 sensitivity is in §7).

For the 2,299 trades this rule actually closed, the exit leg's spread is charged at the entry premium band, because that is the price the exit is modeled at — not the exit's real LTP at the moment the stop would have triggered.

What is not modeled

Personal income tax on trading profit, partial fills and market impact on a large order, and any price movement between two recorded snapshots inside a single 5-minute bar. §8 covers each of these in full, with the direction of the bias stated plainly for each.

05 — Live Deployment & Ongoing Validation

This exit logic was written into config.py (BREAKEVEN_ARM_R = 0.5) and trade_tracker.py, and deployed to both live processes — main_live.py (NIFTY) and main_live_banknifty.py (Bank Nifty, covered in a separate note) — on 2026-08-14. From this date forward, the numbers in §3 stop being a backtest and start being a live measurement.

One fact from the backtest is worth being precise about: across 2,505 real winning trades over six years, zero were ever intercepted by the breakeven rule before reaching target — the "target checked first" precedence in the code never had to matter in this dataset. But a 5-minute reconstructed backtest is not the same thing as a live 30-second poll against real fills, and there's no guarantee that holds going forward. Rather than take the backtest's word for it indefinitely, the live system now does this:

  1. When a trade closes via the breakeven rule, its real P&L is locked in and journaled exactly as before — the breakeven rule's effect on the trading account is final the moment it triggers.
  2. A separate, lightweight shadow record is seeded for the same contract, with no open position and no further P&L impact, and continues watching that contract's live price forward to the original 2R target and stop.
  3. When the shadow resolves — hits the original target, hits the original stop, or the market closes — it's journaled to its own file (breakeven_shadow_journal.jsonl), completely separate from the real trade journal the account's P&L and win-rate figures are computed from.

The question this answers, with real forward data rather than a backtest assumption: of the trades this rule closes early, how many would actually have gone on to hit target? If that fraction turns out to be small, 0.5R remains well-chosen. If it turns out to be large enough to matter, that's the concrete evidence needed to reconsider the threshold — not a hunch, and not a re-run of this same backtest.

Falsification test: is the underlying signal even real?

The breakeven rule only changes when a winning trade gets cut off — it says nothing about whether the entry signal itself carries information. If the scanner's directional calls were noise, buying the opposite of every signal should perform about as well, or better, than following it. Tested directly: every CE the scanner flagged was replaced with the PE at the same strike, and vice versa — same timing, same score, same risk logic, only the direction inverted.

07 — What This Does Not ModelRead this before trusting any number above

01

Personal income tax

Every figure in this note is pre-tax. F&O trading profit in India is taxed as non-speculative business income at the trader's own slab rate, not a flat rate — it depends on total income, regime choice, and other deductions, and cannot be generically baked into a backtest. Reduce every net figure by your own effective rate before treating it as spendable.

02

No real-money execution, and no order-entry delay modeled

Nothing in this system has ever placed a real broker order. It is decision-support: it flags setups and tracks them against live market data, but a human places any actual trade. Every figure here — backtested and live-journal alike — therefore assumes a fill at exactly the price that existed the instant the signal fired.

A real order is punched in seconds later and fills at whatever the market is then. That gap is unmodeled, and it is not symmetric: stops and breakeven exits fire precisely because price is moving against the position, so a delayed exit systematically fills worse, while a delayed entry is roughly a coin flip. The breakeven@0.5R rule depends on exiting near entry, which makes it the rule most exposed to this. As of 2026-08-15 the live system records what the same contract was priced at one and two cycles after every entry and exit, to measure this cost from real observations rather than assume it away — but that dataset starts empty, and no result from it is included here.

03

The breakeven exit fill assumption

For the 2,299 trades this rule closed historically, the exit is modeled at ~entry price, still paying full statutory and spread cost. A real stop order on a fast reversal may fill slightly better or worse than exactly entry. §5's shadow-tracking system is the live system's way of gathering real evidence on this specific question going forward, rather than assuming the backtest model is exactly right.

04

Partial fills & market impact

The spread cost model charges the full quoted spread as a marketable order — the cost of an immediate fill at one lot. It does not model a larger order sweeping multiple book levels, or a fill landing better than the quoted spread. At one lot per trade this is a reasonable approximation; it would not be at meaningfully larger size.

05

The between-cycles blind spot

In this backtest every trigger is checked at reconstructed 5-minute cycles. Live, the resolution is far finer and differs by purpose: new entries are scanned every 30s (POLL_INTERVAL_SECONDS), while target, stop and breakeven on already-open trades are re-checked every 5s (FAST_CHECK_INTERVAL_SECONDS). A spike that touches a level and fully reverses inside one 5-minute reconstructed bar is invisible here even though live tracking would likely have caught it. This cuts both ways — a missed stop, target, or breakeven trigger — and is not corrected for.

06

Non-compounding capital

Every trade is sized against the same flat ₹5,00,000 throughout all six years — the account never grows or shrinks the size of the next bet. A real, compounding account would produce a different (not necessarily better) path: larger absolute swings as equity grows, but also a growing ability to survive a drawdown. The ROI figures above are simple, not compounded.

08 — Status

What is live right now: exactly the strategy described in this note — the 2R target / 0.5R breakeven-arm / original stop / EOD exit logic — running in main_live.py and main_live_banknifty.py as of 2026-08-14. It reached this note validated four independent ways (§6): reproduced from a fresh rerun, confirmed against the real live trade journal, checked for outlier dependence, and checked against a signal-reversal falsification test.

What would change this
The shadow-tracking system described in §5 is now the primary open question — not whether the rule helps on average (§3–§6 answer that), but how often it cuts off a trade that would have gone on to hit target. If that rate turns out to be low, as the six-year backtest suggests, 0.5R stays. If live evidence says otherwise, that evidence — not another backtest of the same historical data — is what should move the threshold next.
